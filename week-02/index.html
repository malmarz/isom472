Finding a project

  • A business problem, not a solution. "We will build an app" is not a problem.
  • Where projects come from. Someone you already know who runs something: a family business, a society, a clinic, a department — someone who will still answer the phone late in the semester. Proximity beats ambition.
  • The four questions to ask a client. What happens today, step by step? What goes wrong, and how often? What do you do when it goes wrong? Who else touches this besides you? Ask all four before proposing anything at all.
  • Do not lead the witness. A question that suggests its own answer gets a polite yes and teaches you nothing.
  • What an accepted proposal contains, and what gets rejected — the five ways a proposal comes back.
  • What you must tell your client. That this is a coursework prototype built by students, that it is publicly visible at a public address, and that it is unsupported after the final week of the semester. Say it in the first meeting, not after the semester ends.

A solution. "We will build an ordering app for a bakery." Nothing here can be wrong, so nothing here can be checked.

A problem. "Orders arrive on WhatsApp, nobody records them, and about four a week are lost." It names what happens today, what goes wrong, and how often.

Working with AI

  • Why you must hold the concepts. You cannot phrase an instruction for a concept you do not have, and you cannot judge an answer you do not understand. Quotas also run out, usually mid-phase, and part of this work is yours to do by hand.
  • Prompting, in three moves. Give it the context it cannot see — the file, the schema, the error. Say what "done" looks like before it starts. Ask for the plan before the code.
  • How to verify. Run it on the real path, not the happy path. Feed it bad input: empty, wrong type, a duplicate. Read it, and be able to say what each part does. The failure mode is not code that breaks; it is code that looks right and is confidently wrong.
  • You own what you ship. The agent wrote it; you are answerable for it, including the parts you did not type. Misconduct here is the inability to explain your own system, not the use of a tool.

Every pull request carries a declaration, in one of these two forms:

AI-Assisted: <tool> / <what it produced> / <what you changed>

AI-Assisted: none

Both are fully legitimate. Not declaring is the offence.

The repository

  • Six words. A repository is the team's folder plus its entire history. A branch is a line of work that does not disturb anyone else's. A commit is a saved point carrying your name and a message. A pull request says "here is my work, please look at it before it becomes real". A review is a teammate reading it and saying what they checked. A merge makes it part of the real thing. Branch › commit › pull request › review › merge is the whole working method of this course.
  • Three rules, and only three. One branch per story, named after the task. Nobody merges their own work — a teammate reads it and says what they checked. The Client Lead accepts: a story is not done because the person who built it says so.
  • The commit form — a type, a scope, what changed, and the story ID, so the work traces back to the board.

Before the second class

  • Hold your first client meeting. Read the interview article below before it, not after it.
  • Draft docs/proposal.md in your team repository, in the eight required sections and nothing else. The required shape is on the project proposal page, with a worked example that would be accepted and three versions that would be returned.
  • Publish the same content as a page from your repository, so it is reachable at its own address. You present from that address. It is checked as done or not done, and it is not graded on how it looks — spend this week talking to your client, not making it pretty. The steps are on publishing from your repository.

Bring your laptop. The class opens with your repository on screen.

The second class

The Phase 1 delivery. The class opens by walking branch › commit › pull request together on screen, with your own repository in front of you. Then each team presents its proposal from the live public address and takes questions from the room.

Proposals are reviewed in front of everyone. Every team hears the other teams' problems, which is the point of running it that way.
