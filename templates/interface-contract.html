The interface contract card

The required shape. Fill in this shape, not a document of your own design.

ISOM 472 › The interface contract card

A contract card is an agreement, not a document. Half a page. It says what one of you promises to hand another, in writing, before either of you starts building. If a card takes you more than ten minutes to write, you are writing the wrong thing.

How to use this page. Copy the block in Part 2 into your team repository, once per boundary, under contracts/. Parts 1, 3, 4 and 5 are teaching, not template. Your contract is the block in Part 2 and nothing else.

How it is agreed. The card is added to the repository in a pull request, and the other party's review is the agreement. A card nobody reviewed is a plan, not a contract.

Part 1 — What a card is, and when you write one

The failure it exists to prevent

One member builds the screen and expects customerName. One member builds the data behind it and returns customer_full_name. Both finish on time. Neither half works. Two days to find it, ten minutes to fix it, and it happens to somebody every single term.

Nothing about that is a failure of effort, which is why effort will not prevent it. The only thing that prevents it is agreeing the name before either person starts.

The order everything is decided in

Contracts are written at the start of a sprint, when stories are assigned, and always before either person builds. They sit at a fixed point in a fixed order:

# You decide Then
1 The user story — who benefits, what they want, why it matters before any of it is built
2 The acceptance criteria — how a teammate checks it is done
3 The schema — what the system remembers, and under what names
4 The contracts for the boundaries the schema does not settle one card each
5 Screens and code last, and only now

The schema is itself a contract — the biggest one. It is the single agreement every member depends on, and it is agreed the same way: written down, reviewed, and not changed by one person quietly. Every card in Part 2 exists only for the boundaries the schema leaves open — what passes between your parts, which the tables do not say.

How many cards, and where

One card per boundary, not per pair of people. Two members whose work meets in three places write three cards, not one; two members who happen to sit together and share nothing write none.

Typically three to six cards a sprint, at these boundaries:

  • at the schema — what a table's rows mean and which fields may be empty;
  • between a screen and the data behind it;
  • between two stories, where one produces what the other consumes.

The rule: if you cannot name who consumes it, you do not need a card. A card with no named consumer is documentation, and documentation is not what this is for.

Who writes it

The two people whose work meets, together, in one sitting, before either opens an editor. Not the team lead on everyone's behalf, and not one person who then informs the other. Both names go on the card because both of you are bound by it.

Part 2 — The card

Copy this. One file per boundary, named after the boundary.

## Contract: <the_name_of_what_is_passed>

**What is passed:** one sentence, in plain words.
**Produced by:** <role or story> — <member>
**Consumed by:** <role or story> — <member>

| Field | Type | Required? | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| `field_name` | text / whole number / decimal / date / true-false | required | what it means, and any limit |
| `field_name` | | optional | say what happens when it is absent |

**Example record**

```
field_name:  a real value, not a placeholder
field_name:  a real value
```

**What comes back:** one line. Name the fields if there is more than one.

**When a field is missing or wrong:** what the other side gets, and what is *not* saved.

The four rules of a card

  1. The field names are the contract. Not the types, not the notes — the names. Your agent generates each half of this boundary in a separate session, and it will invent a different plausible name each time unless it is told: phone, phone_number, mobile, contact are all reasonable and only one of them works. Write the names down and never let either side “improve” one alone.
  2. Say required or optional for every field. A field with no answer in that column will be treated as required by one side and optional by the other. That is not a rename to fix — it is a rule change, and it costs more.
  3. One example record, with real values. A card without an example is a card two people read differently. The example is what makes 2025-09-18 and 18/09/2025 a settled question.
  4. Never say how either side builds it. The name, the fields, what is required, and what comes back on failure are fixed. How you build your half is nobody else's business. A contract that dictates implementation is not a contract.

Four columns and an example record. A teammate should be able to check a card in under a minute — that is the test of whether it is the right size. No tool is required, and no formal notation: if you find yourself writing anything that looks like code, you have gone too far.

Part 3 — Two worked cards

Same charity as the proposal example: Al-Nahda Charitable Society, Jahra branch — monthly food baskets, a family register, and a check that stops a family collecting twice in one month.

These two cards fit together. One is a screen handing a new registration to the data behind it. The other is one story handing something to another story. They are deliberately ordinary and deliberately small — that is what a correct card looks like.

Card 1 — the registration screen hands a new family to the data

## Contract: new_family_registration

**What is passed:** the details a volunteer types on the registration screen, sent once when
the form is submitted, to be stored in the family register.
**Produced by:** the registration screen story — the screen owner
**Consumed by:** the family register story — the data owner

| Field | Type | Required? | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| `family_name` | text | required | as written on the paper form; 3–80 characters |
| `household_size` | whole number | required | 1 or more; the number fed by one basket |
| `area` | text | required | one of the branch's area names, spelled exactly |
| `phone` | text | required | 8 digits, digits only — no spaces, no `+`, no `00965` |
| `eligibility_reviewed_on` | date | required | `YYYY-MM-DD`; the date the form was last checked |
| `notes` | text | optional | free text; absent and empty both mean "nothing to note" |

**Example record**

```
family_name:             Al-Otaibi household, Block 4
household_size:          7
area:                    Naseem
phone:                   66123456
eligibility_reviewed_on: 2025-09-18
notes:
```

**What comes back:** `family_id`, a whole number, newly created.

**When a field is missing or wrong:** nothing is saved — no half-created family — and the screen
gets back one message naming the first field at fault, for example
`household_size must be a whole number of 1 or more`. The screen shows that message next to the
field and keeps everything the volunteer typed.

Card 2 — the register story asks the duplicate-check story

## Contract: duplicate_family_check

**What is passed:** a candidate family, before it is written to the register, to be checked
against families already registered at this branch.
**Produced by:** the family register story — the data owner
**Consumed by:** the duplicate-check story — the duplicate-check owner

| Field | Type | Required? | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| `family_name` | text | required | as typed; matching ignores case and extra spaces |
| `phone` | text | required | 8 digits, digits only — same rule as registration |
| `area` | text | required | one of the branch's area names |
| `exclude_family_id` | whole number | optional | when an existing family is being edited, its own id, so it is never matched against itself; absent when registering a new family |

**Example record**

```
family_name:       Al-Otaibi household, Block 4
phone:             66123456
area:              Naseem
exclude_family_id:
```

**What comes back:** three fields —
`is_duplicate` (true-false, always present);
`matched_family_id` (whole number, present only when `is_duplicate` is true);
`matched_on` (text, either `phone` or `name_and_area`, present only when `is_duplicate` is true).

**When a field is missing or wrong:** the check is not performed and one message comes back, for
example `phone must be 8 digits`. **`is_duplicate` is never guessed** — a check that could not
run never comes back as `false`, because the register would then save a duplicate believing it
had been checked.

Two things to notice, because they are the parts teams leave out. The optional field carries a sentence saying what its absence means — that sentence is why it can be optional at all. And the failure line says what does not happen, not only what message appears: nothing is saved, and no verdict is invented.

Part 4 — The same cards, done badly

Five versions of the same two boundaries that will cost the team a merge. Each one is annotated with what specifically is wrong and what it costs.

Bad card A — prose, with no example

“The registration screen will send the family's details to the database, which will save the family and send back a confirmation. If there is a problem it will show an error.”

What is wrong: not one field is named. “Details”, “confirmation” and “a problem” are not names, and nobody can check any of it in a minute — or at all.

What it costs: exactly the failure this exists to prevent. The screen sends familyName, the data layer expects family_name, both halves pass their own checks, and the two of you spend two days on a ten-minute rename with a delivery session in the way.

Bad card B — field names with no required-or-optional

family_name, household_size, area, phone, eligibility_reviewed_on, notes — six names, one per line, and nothing else.

What is wrong: the column that decides behaviour is missing. Six names look like an agreement and settle nothing.

What it costs: the data owner assumes phone is required and rejects every family without one. The branch has families without a phone. This is not a rename — it is a rule change on both sides, and possibly a schema change, which is why it costs more than case A even though it looks closer to right.

Bad card C — written after the code

Written the night before the delivery session by reading whichever half was finished first, and describing it.

What is wrong: a contract agreed after the fact is a description of a bug. It also silently declares that whichever half happened to be built first is the correct one — a decision nobody made, and usually the wrong one.

What it costs: the merge already failed; this card just records the failure in a nicer format. And it cannot be reviewed, because there is nothing left to disagree with.

Bad card D — written by one person, never reviewed

A correct, well-shaped card, pushed straight to the main branch. The other party never opened the pull request.

What is wrong: the review is the agreement. One person's card is one person's plan, and the other party is not bound by a plan they never read.

What it costs: the unreviewed half gets built to different names, and the author is the one who has to change, having been right all along. Nothing is more expensive than being right in a file nobody opened.

Bad card E — over-specified

Thirty rows — every column of the families table, including created_at, updated_at and six columns the screen never sees — plus a paragraph saying the check “loops through all families, calls the search function twice, and wraps it in a try/except”.

What is wrong: two faults at once. It dictates implementation, which the contract must leave open; and it lists twenty-four fields that never cross the boundary, so the six that do are buried.

What it costs: nobody can check it in a minute, so nobody checks it. And the moment either side changes an internal detail — which your agent will do in the next session, unasked — the card is wrong, the team learns cards are unreliable, and they stop writing them. That is the most expensive outcome on this page.

Part 5 — A good contract is a prompt

This is the payoff, and it is why the discipline is worth keeping.

You do not write the code; you direct an agent that does. The agent builds each half in a separate session and remembers nothing between them. A card is the smallest complete specification for one half — so you do not paraphrase it to the agent, you paste it.

The producing side

Build the registration screen for the Jahra branch family register.

This is the interface contract for the boundary. Send exactly these field names, exactly these types. Do not rename anything, do not add a field, do not drop the optional one.

[paste Card 1 here, whole]

Build only the screen side: the form, validation before sending, and displaying the message that comes back. Do not build the storage.

The consuming side — a different member, a different session

Build the family register storage for the Jahra branch.

This is the interface contract for the boundary. Accept exactly these field names, exactly these types. Return family_id on success, and on a bad field save nothing and return the one message named below.

[paste Card 1 here, whole]

Build only the storage side. Do not build the screen.

Two people, two sessions, two agents that never spoke to each other — and the halves fit, because the same six names were in both prompts. That is the entire trick.

Three habits that follow from it:

  • Paste the card in every new session. The agent does not remember the last one, and it will cheerfully invent phone_number again.
  • When the card changes, both halves are re-run. A change agreed with one person is not a change. Edit the card in a pull request, get the review, then rebuild.
  • When the agent proposes a better name, take it to the other party, not to the code. The suggestion is often good. Acting on it alone never is.

One note on sources

Where this page and the class slides differ, the slides win. Four consequences worth naming:

  • The slides fix four things — the name, the fields, what is required, and what comes back on failure — and the five-line example on the slide adds agreed by. The card here is the same four things with two additions: a type and a note column, and agreed by split into produced by and consumed by, so the two signatures are unambiguous.
  • The card also names what comes back on success. The slides fix only the failure line; a boundary that returns an id or a verdict cannot be agreed without it.
  • The slides ask for contracts for every boundary between members. Written per boundary rather than per pair, that is typically three to six cards a sprint — and the consumer test (“if you cannot name who consumes it, you do not need a card”) is how you tell a real boundary from a handoff that needs nothing written down.
  • The schema is a contract is stated here and taught in the class immediately after the contracts class. The ordering is the same in both: story, criteria, schema, contracts, code.
